The ferry left at the hour when the light goes thin and silver over the water, and the passengers stood along the rail as if they were waiting to be told something; nobody spoke, and the gulls, who are never quiet, seemed to have agreed to say nothing either.

She watched the coast fall away behind them, the white houses first and then the church and then the long grey line of the breakwater, until only the lighthouse remained, patient on its spit of rock, turning its slow attention across the sea as it had every evening of her life.

It occurred to her, somewhere past the second buoy, that she had never once seen the harbour from this side of the water; she had always been the one who stayed, the one who waved, the one who climbed the stair at dusk and wound the clockwork while other people went away.

The wind came round from the north, carrying the smell of rain and diesel and cold weed, and she pulled her collar close and thought about the lamp, and about who would wind it tonight, and whether the stair would know the difference in the sound of the footsteps.
