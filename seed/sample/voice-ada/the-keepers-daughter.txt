In the years before the storm, the keeper's daughter learned the lamp the way other children learn the names of birds: slowly, by repetition, until the knowledge settled beneath thought and became a part of the body itself.

Her mother taught her to read the weather in the colour of the evening, to listen for the particular groan the clockwork made when it wanted oil, and to count the stair aloud so that the stone would know she was coming; it was nonsense, of course, and she knew it was nonsense, and she counted anyway.

There were nights when the fog came in so thick that the beam seemed to stop a hand's width from the glass, held there like breath on a cold window, and on those nights her mother would sit beside the lamp until morning and say nothing at all, as though silence itself were a kind of watch.

She remembered those nights long after her mother had gone inland to the small warm house with the garden; she remembered the quiet, and the clockwork, and the sense that the whole dark coast was listening for the light, and trusting it, and asking nothing more.
